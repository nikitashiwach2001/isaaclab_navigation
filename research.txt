a planner + a path-following policy is a standard, proven setup, and it fixes the freeze: the planner hands the robot waypoints that are always in open space, so the policy never has to figure out a detour around a wall on its own.

But moving-objects question:

Two jobs, split clearly:

Planner = the things that don't move: the maze walls, and a blocker that has stopped. It re-draws the route now and then.
Policy = follow the waypoints + dodge the things that do move. Runs every instant.
Will the planner handle moving objects? No — here's why (simple):
A planner is slow. It draws a route, the robot follows it, then it re-draws — maybe a few times a second. A walking person moves faster than that. By the time the planner finishes a new route around the person, the person has already moved. So it routes the robot around where the person was, not where they are — and the robot can drive right into them.

Planners are good at things that don't move. They're bad at things that do move.

So who handles moving objects? The policy.
The policy runs every instant and reacts fast — that's exactly the moving-obstacle dodging we already do well (~95%). Good news: that skill is kept, not thrown away. The planner just adds route-finding on top.

The clean rule:

A person crosses your path → the policy briefly steps off the path, dodges, rejoins. No replan.
Something stops in your path and stays → the planner re-draws the route around it.
(How does it tell them apart? Simple: if a detected object hasn't moved for a couple of seconds → treat it as static → replan. Still moving → leave it to the policy.)
"Finetune the policy to follow the path" — what that means simply:
Right now we give the policy the final goal. Instead, give it the next waypoint as its goal — a near, always-reachable point. The policy already knows "go to the goal, dodge obstacles" — it barely changes. And because waypoints are always in open space, the freeze disappears. Light finetune, keeps the dodging skill.

Bottom line:

Planner handles walls + stopped blockers. 
Policy handles moving people + smooth driving. (already have it)
"Will it handle moving objects?" — yes, but thanks to the policy, not the planner.
One important note: if you go the planner route, you probably don't need the GRU. The GRU was a way to make the policy itself smart enough to go around blockers — the planner does that job instead. So pick one path, not both. For a maze demo with goals behind walls, the planner is the more direct fit.

Now two questions.

Q1: How does it keep following waypoints to the last one?

A small piece of glue code sits between the planner and the policy — call it the waypoint manager. It's not learned, it's not the policy, it's just a few lines of plain code.

The planner gives a list of points: W1, W2, … Wn (Wn = the real goal).
The manager feeds the policy one point at a time as its "goal." It starts with W1.
Each step it checks: is the robot close to the current target? When yes → switch the target to the next one.
Repeat until Wn. Done.
The policy never even knows waypoints exist. It always just sees "a goal" and drives to it — the manager keeps sliding the target forward. Like a carrot on a stick.

Best style: don't force the robot to exactly touch each waypoint. Keep the target a short distance ahead of the robot along the path — a sliding carrot. Smoother, and one briefly-blocked spot won't hard-stop the whole sequence. And yes — no real retrain needed; to the policy a waypoint just looks like a near goal, which it already handles.

Q2: A moving object sits on a waypoint — what happens?

First, the reassuring part: the policy already dodges moving obstacles. It will NOT drive straight into one sitting on a waypoint — we know this, its dynamic-crash rate is only ~2-3%. So it goes around it.

It won't "wait" — and that's actually fine for a moving object. The object is moving; by the time the robot navigates over there, the object has moved on. The robot dodges, the object passes, the robot continues. For a moving person you want dodging, not waiting — and we're already good at it.

And here's the thing — while that spot is briefly blocked, the robot naturally mills and dodges near it and doesn't advance the carrot. That is "waiting it out" — just as a side effect, no explicit wait behavior needed.

So a moving object on a waypoint → handled, no change needed.

The real problem is not a moving object — it's an object that STOPS on a waypoint and stays. Then that waypoint is permanently unreachable, and the robot would circle there forever.

Fix — one simple rule: if the robot can't get past a point for a few seconds → the planner marks that spot "blocked" → replans a new path around it. (Same "stopped thing → replan" rule from before.)

Bottom line:

Moving object on a waypoint → policy dodges, object passes, robot continues. ✅ No change needed.
Stopped object on a waypoint → planner replans around it. ⚠️ This is the one case you must build the replan rule for

Yes — you can, and it's a reasonable "later" step. Just be clear-eyed about it.

How you'd do it: finetune the policy (now a waypoint-follower) on scenes with more, faster, denser moving obstacles than it trained on. Training on harder moving-obstacle situations is the most direct way to make it better at them.

A bonus in the new setup: as a waypoint-follower, the policy's job is simpler — the planner handles the routes, so the policy no longer has to also fight the freeze/route problem. That frees up "spare capacity" it can spend on getting better at dodging.

Honest limit: the policy is near its ceiling — ~95%, moving-obstacle failures already only ~2–3%. A finetune will likely shave that down a bit, not jump it to near-perfect. The big leaps in moving-obstacle skill would need the GRU (memory, to better predict where a person is heading) — and we're dropping the GRU. So: expect a modest polish, not a transformation.

Most important — the timing: do this last, not now.

First build the planner + waypoint system, run it with the current policy as-is.
Run the demo and see what actually fails.
If moving obstacles turn out to be the real weak point → then finetune for it.
If the weak point is something else (planner, replan timing, waypoint spacing) → finetuning the policy won't help.
Don't finetune before you know it's the bottleneck. Build the pipeline, watch it fail, then fix the thing that actually broke.
